# Vision: the Sky of Questions

*Русская версия: [ru/VISION.md](ru/VISION.md)*

> "A mathematician, like a painter or poet, is a maker of patterns. If his patterns are
> more permanent than theirs, it is because they are made with ideas." — G. H. Hardy, quoted by Lockhart

## Where we start from

Paul Lockhart's essay *A Mathematician's Lament* is not about teaching methods. It is about
the fact that **mathematics itself has been removed** from school mathematics, leaving an
empty shell of formulas, terminology and exercises. The ideas this project stands on:

| Lockhart says | What it means for us |
|---|---|
| Mathematics is an art, "the music of reason". Simple is beautiful. | Every adventure is a small work of art: one simple question, one beautiful idea. No "covering topics". |
| "I'm just *playing*. That's what math is — wondering, playing, amusing yourself with your imagination." | The child is always doing something with their hands: pulling, cutting, turning, playing. Play first, then a question, then an idea. |
| "A good problem is something you don't know how to solve." | We ask the question before giving the answer and let the child stay in not-knowing for a while. Hints come only when the child is stuck — one at a time. |
| "Mathematics is the art of explanation." Not *what*, but *why*. | The main thing a child produces is **their own explanation in their own voice**. Every adventure ends with an invitation to explain. |
| "My definition, my theorem, my proof." | The child's own questions become stars in their sky. Their ideas are kept next to the question. |
| "Art is not a race." The ladder myth. | A map, not a ladder. No levels, points, grade-stars, timers or rankings. No "you're falling behind". |
| Word problems about Maria's age are fake. People want *fantasy*. | We don't pretend to be "useful". The link to the world is through beauty and pattern (circles, symmetry, games), not swimming pools and percentages. |
| "Play games! Chess and Go, Hex and Backgammon, Sprouts and Nim..." | Games are full members of the sky: each hides a secret that can be uncovered. |
| No "facts", only stories. The history of mathematics is the history of people and questions. | We tell *who* came up with an idea and *how* (Lockhart's student, Thales, Archimedes, the Babylonians), not "memorise Theorem 9.5". |
| Needless terminology does harm. "I'd be happy to use 'corner' or even 'pigpen'." | Terms appear only when talking without them gets awkward — and later for older children. First "a corner like a sheet of paper", then "a right angle". |
| A teacher is a real person in an honest intellectual relationship. "Just talk to them! And more importantly, listen to them!" | Lumo, the keeper, is not an examiner but a companion: he wonders, doubts, and honestly says "I don't know". |

## What we are making

**Lumo — Sky of Questions** is an interactive, voice-first portal for children aged about
4–12 (with three age bands). The home screen is a night sky where every star is a question:

* **big stars** — adventures: interactive scenes where the child finds an idea together with Lumo;
* **small violet stars** — open questions we do *not* have an answer to. Lumo "hangs them
  in the sky" at the end of adventures. The child can record an idea for them;
* **faraway stars in nebulae** — questions from the edge of the sky: adventures still being
  built (the diagonal of a cube, do primes go on forever, is infinity a number?);
* **green stars** — the child's own questions, recorded in their own voice.

The keeper of the sky is **Lumo**, a small living light (see [KEEPER.md](KEEPER.md)).

## Principles (every new adventure is checked against them)

1. **Everything by voice.** The child does not need to read. Everything that must be
   understood, Lumo says. Everything that must be pressed is a picture. Tapping Lumo repeats
   the last line. Subtitles are only an option for grown-ups and hard-of-hearing children.
2. **Question before answer.** First "I wonder how much...?", then a guess, then play, then the idea.
3. **No "wrong".** There are "interesting guesses", "hmm, can't see it yet", "brave ideas".
   A mistake is a normal part of discovery.
4. **One idea — one adventure.** 5–10 minutes. Beauty in simplicity.
5. **Hands before words.** The child moves, cuts and turns things. We show, not tell.
6. **Why, not what.** Every adventure ends with understanding *why*, and an invitation to
   explain it in one's own words.
7. **An open ending.** Every adventure leaves a new question we have no ready answer to.
8. **A map, not a ladder.** No locks, levels or "finish this first". Links between stars
   mean "this question leads to that one", never prerequisites.
9. **No points, timers or rankings.** The only "reward": a star lights up, and Lumo glows
   when the child sees the idea.
10. **Patience.** A hint is said at most once; after that Lumo waits. Nothing nags.
11. **Honesty.** Lumo doesn't pretend to know everything, and doesn't pretend mathematics
    is "for counting change".

## Why a living portal, not a set of tasks

* **The sky grows.** Every adventure is a separate module (a script + lines in each
  language). New stars are added without touching the rest. Open questions are ready-made
  "requests" for future adventures.
* **Each child has their own sky.** The child's questions and ideas become their stars.
  Coming back, they see their history of discoveries, not a completion percentage.
* **Adventures can be replayed differently.** The free play at the end (pull the tip out of
  the box, move the point off the circle, a new game rule) is not "practice" but an
  invitation to explore further.
* **Grows with the child.** The same adventure speaks differently to a 5-year-old and a
  11-year-old: simpler words for little ones, real names and a bit of history for older ones.
* **A community of authors.** An adventure is written in a small scene language
  ([AUTHORING.md](AUTHORING.md)) — a teacher, a mathematician, a parent or a student can
  write one. A translation is just another file of lines.

## What we deliberately do NOT do

* follow the school curriculum "by grade" or prepare for tests;
* gamify with points, badges, streaks or competition;
* make "cutesy" mathematics for the sake of cuteness (Lockhart on "Mr. C and Mrs. A");
* make children read, write or fill anything in;
* send the child's voice or data anywhere (everything stays on the device).

## Who else it is for

* **Parents** sit nearby: they hear what the child hears and can join the conversation — Lumo
  himself suggests "tell someone next to you". The album of the child's recordings is for them.
* **Teachers** can show adventures on a big screen and argue with the class — and then write their own.
